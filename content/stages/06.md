Everything so far has been measured in nanoseconds. This chapter is measured in
**milliseconds** — a million times slower — and that single fact reorganises
every decision in it.

It is also where redundancy stops being about one flipped bit and starts being
about a whole drive dying while you are using it.

<!-- block: prose -->
## Why a disk is slow, in three parts

A magnetic disk stores bits as magnetised regions on rotating platters, read by
a head that moves in and out. Getting to a particular block costs three things,
and only one of them is the actual reading:

<!-- block: code -->
Access time  =  seek time  +  rotational latency  +  transfer time

seek        move the head to the right track      — MILLISECONDS
rotational  wait for the sector to come round     — milliseconds
transfer    actually read the bits                — fast

<!-- block: prose -->
**Seek dominates, and it is mechanical.** No amount of clever electronics makes
an arm move faster. That is why sequential access is enormously cheaper than
random access on a disk, and why every layer above — the file system, the
database, the operating system's scheduler — is built around avoiding seeks.

Rotational latency follows from the spin rate. A 7200 rpm disk turns once every
8.33 ms, so on average you wait half of that — about 4.17 ms — for the sector
you want to arrive.

<!-- block: callout -->
**Put it against Stage 04.** A cache hit was 2 ns. A disk seek is around 4 ms.

That is a factor of **two million**. If a cache hit took one second, a disk
access would take three weeks.

Every design decision in this chapter follows from that ratio, and so does most
of Stage 08.

<!-- block: prose -->
## How the surface is organised

Platters are divided into **tracks** — concentric rings — and each track into
**sectors**, the smallest addressable unit. The same track number across all
platters forms a **cylinder**, which matters because reading a whole cylinder
costs one seek.

The interesting question is what to do about the fact that outer tracks are
physically longer than inner ones.

**Constant angular velocity (CAV)** puts the *same number of sectors* on every
track. The disk spins at one rate and the geometry is simple — but the outer
tracks store bits at a lower density than they could, wasting capacity.

**Multiple zone recording** divides the surface into zones and gives outer zones
*more* sectors per track. More capacity, and more complexity: the transfer rate
now depends on which zone you are reading.

<!-- block: callout -->
**A recurring shape.** CAV is simple and wasteful; zone recording is efficient
and complicated. That is the same trade as direct-mapped versus set-associative
cache in Stage 04, and the same as bus versus point-to-point in Stage 03.

Notice you are starting to recognise these before they are explained.

<!-- block: prose -->
## RAID: what to do when a disk dies

A single disk has a failure rate. Put fifty in a machine and one is failing
somewhere all the time. RAID is the organised answer.

<!-- block: quote source="ch-06.md 6.2" -->
RAID is a set of physical disk drives viewed by the operating system as a single logical drive.

<!-- block: quote source="ch-06.md 6.2" -->
Data are distributed across the physical drives of an array in a scheme known as striping, described subsequently.

<!-- block: prose -->
And the third characteristic is the one that earns the name — redundant capacity
used to recover from failure.

The book is careful about something students routinely get wrong:

<!-- block: quote source="ch-06.md 6.2" -->
These levels do not imply a hierarchical relationship but designate different design architectures

<!-- block: prose -->
**RAID 5 is not "better than" RAID 1.** They answer different questions.

<!-- block: prose -->
## The levels you must be able to choose between

<!-- block: code -->
LEVEL 0 — striping, no redundancy
  Data split across N disks. Fast, and N times more likely to fail.
  NOT redundant despite the name. Use when the data is disposable.

LEVEL 1 — mirroring
  Every block written twice. Simplest recovery, fastest rebuild.
  Costs 50% of your capacity. Reads can be served by either copy.

LEVEL 2 — bit-level striping with Hamming
  ECC across drives. Obsolete: drives now detect their own errors,
  so the machinery is redundant. Worth knowing WHY it died.

LEVEL 3 — byte-level striping, one parity disk
  One parity drive serves all. Any single drive can be rebuilt.
  The parity disk is a bottleneck: every write touches it.

LEVEL 4 — block-level striping, one parity disk
  As 3, but larger units. Same bottleneck.

LEVEL 5 — block striping, DISTRIBUTED parity
  Parity spread across all drives, so no single write bottleneck.
  Survives one failure. The common choice.

LEVEL 6 — two independent parity schemes
  Survives TWO simultaneous failures. Costs more, writes slower.
  Chosen when a rebuild takes long enough that a second disk
  might plausibly die during it — which, on large modern drives,
  it might.

<!-- block: callout -->
**How to defend a choice**, which is what the exam and the project both want:

- Data is disposable and speed is everything → **0**
- Small array, rebuild speed matters most → **1**
- General purpose, mostly reads → **5**
- Large drives, long rebuilds, cannot risk a second failure → **6**

Naming the *stress* — read-heavy, write-heavy, rebuild window, capacity budget —
is what turns an opinion into an argument.

<!-- block: prose -->
## Optical, solid state, and tape

**Optical** — CD, DVD, Blu-ray — reads pits and lands with a laser. Each
generation used a shorter wavelength, which is what allowed smaller pits and
more capacity. Cheap to mass-produce, slow, and largely displaced.

**Solid-state drives** have no moving parts, so **seek time effectively
vanishes** — the three-part access time above collapses to one part. That is not
a faster disk; it is a different device with different failure behaviour: flash
cells wear out after a finite number of erase cycles, so an SSD's controller
spends its life spreading writes evenly.

**Tape** is still here, and for one reason: cost per byte at scale, with no
power draw at rest. It is strictly sequential, so restoring one file means
winding to it. That is exactly why it is an archive medium and not a working
one — and why `RUNBOOK.md` insists a backup is not real until you have restored
from it.

## What to carry into Stage 07

You can now compute a disk access time, explain why sequential beats random by
orders of magnitude, and choose a RAID level with a reason attached.

The obvious next question: while the processor waits four milliseconds for a
