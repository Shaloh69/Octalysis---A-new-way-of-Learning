## Four generations, and the one thing that changed

The history is worth an hour not because dates matter but because **each
generation solved the previous one's limiting problem**, and the limiting
problem keeps moving.

- **Vacuum tubes** (ENIAC, IAS). Enormous, hot, and unreliable — a machine with
  eighteen thousand tubes fails often. The stored-program idea arrives here.
- **Transistors.** Smaller, cooler, more reliable. The gain is *density*.
- **Integrated circuits.** Many transistors on one piece of silicon. The gain is
  density again, and now it compounds — this is where Moore's observation starts
  to bite.
- **Later generations.** Not a new device but a new *organization*: pipelines,
  caches, superscalar issue, and finally multiple cores.

Notice the shape. The first three generations got faster by making the switch
better. **The fourth got faster by changing how the parts are arranged** — which
is precisely the organization/architecture distinction from Stage 01, playing
out over sixty years.

<!-- block: prose -->
## Why the free lunch ended

For decades the dominant source of speed was simply a faster clock and more
transistors. That stopped, and the textbook names why:

<!-- block: quote source="ch-02.md 2.1" -->
As the density of logic and the clock speed on a chip increase, so does the power density (Watts/cm2). The difficulty of dissipating the heat generated on high-density, high-speed chips is becoming a serious design issue

<!-- block: prose -->
Two more limits arrive with it. **RC delay** — the wires between transistors
have resistance and capacitance, and as they get thinner the delay through them
grows, so at some point the *wiring* is slower than the switching. And **memory
latency**, which has improved far more slowly than processor speed, so a faster
processor spends a larger fraction of its life waiting.

The industry's answer was to stop making one core faster and start putting
several on a chip. **That answer has a ceiling, and the next section is how to
compute it.**

<!-- block: prose -->
## Amdahl's Law — what parallelism cannot buy you

Gene Amdahl, 1967. Take a program where a fraction **f** is perfectly
parallelisable and the remaining **(1 − f)** is inherently sequential. Run it on
**N** processors:

<!-- block: code -->
                    1
Speedup  =  ─────────────────────
            (1 − f)  +   f / N

<!-- block: prose -->
Two conclusions, and the textbook states them plainly:

<!-- block: quote source="ch-02.md 2.3" -->
When f is small, the use of parallel processors has little effect.

<!-- block: quote source="ch-02.md 2.3" -->
As N approaches infinity, speedup is bound by 1/(1 - f ), so that there are diminishing returns for using more processors.

<!-- block: prose -->
Read that second one carefully, because it is the sentence that governs the rest
of the course.

If 10% of your program is sequential, then **no number of processors will ever
make it more than 10× faster.** Not a thousand cores. Not a million. The
sequential tenth is a floor, and you cannot buy your way under it.

**Worked example.** A program is 95% parallelisable. On 8 processors:

<!-- block: code -->
Speedup = 1 / (0.05 + 0.95/8)
        = 1 / (0.05 + 0.11875)
        = 1 / 0.16875
        = 5.93×

Eight processors. Not eight times faster — 5.93 times.
The other 2.07 went to the 5% you could not parallelise.

<!-- block: callout -->
**Amdahl is not the last word, and the book says so.** It calls the pessimistic
reading into question: a server handling many independent clients, or a database
splitting work across a huge dataset, has far more genuinely parallel work than
the law's single-program framing suggests.

Use Amdahl to answer *"how much faster will this one program get?"* — not
*"is multicore worth it?"* Those are different questions and it only answers the
first.

<!-- block: prose -->
## Little's Law — the one that describes a queue

Amdahl is about speedup. Little's Law is about **what is in the system right
now**, and it applies to anything with arrivals, waiting and departures — a
memory queue, an I/O request pipeline, a lecture theatre.

<!-- block: code -->
L  =  λ  ×  W

L = average number of items in the system
λ = average arrival rate
W = average time an item spends in the system

<!-- block: prose -->
Its usefulness is that it holds with almost no assumptions. If you can measure
any two, you get the third for free — and you will use it in **Stage 07** for
I/O queues and again in **Stage 12** for pipeline occupancy.

## Measuring a processor: clock speed, CPI, MIPS

**Clock speed alone tells you almost nothing.** A 3 GHz processor is not faster
than a 2 GHz one if it takes twice as many cycles per instruction. The bridge
between the two is **CPI** — cycles per instruction.

The book gives the MIPS rate directly:

<!-- block: quote source="ch-02.md 2.4" -->
A common measure of performance for a processor is the rate at which instructions are executed, expressed as millions of instructions per second (MIPS), referred to as the MIPS rate.

<!-- block: code -->
        f
MIPS = ──────────────
       CPI × 10⁶

f   = clock frequency in Hz
CPI = average cycles per instruction

<!-- block: prose -->
And CPI itself is a weighted average over the instruction mix — you cannot get
it from a datasheet, because it depends on what the program does:

<!-- block: code -->
CPI = Σ (CPIᵢ × fractionᵢ)

<!-- block: prose -->
**Worked example**, following the textbook's own: 2 million instructions on a
400 MHz processor, with this mix —

<!-- block: code -->
Instruction type            CPI   Mix
─────────────────────────────────────
Arithmetic and logic          1   60%
Load/store with cache hit     2   18%
Branch                        4   12%
Memory reference, cache miss  8   10%

CPI = (1 × 0.60) + (2 × 0.18) + (4 × 0.12) + (8 × 0.10)
    = 0.60 + 0.36 + 0.48 + 0.80
    = 2.24

MIPS = 400 × 10⁶ / (2.24 × 10⁶)
     = 178.6 MIPS

<!-- block: callout -->
**Look at where the time went.** Cache misses are 10% of the instructions and
contribute **0.80 of the 2.24** — over a third of the CPI. Fix the cache and you
move that number more than any clock increase would.

That is the whole argument for **Stage 04**, made with arithmetic instead of
assertion. Keep this example; you will meet it again.

<!-- block: prose -->
## MFLOPS, and a second look at Amdahl

For numerical work, instructions are the wrong unit — what matters is
floating-point operations:

<!-- block: code -->
              floating-point operations executed
MFLOPS  =  ─────────────────────────────────────
               execution time × 10⁶

<!-- block: prose -->
And here Amdahl reappears in a form you will meet in the project. The textbook's
own case: a program spends 40% of its time in floating-point operations, and a
new design speeds that module up by a factor of **K**.

<!-- block: code -->
                    1
Speedup  =  ───────────────────
             0.6  +  0.4 / K

<!-- block: prose -->
Push K to infinity — an *infinitely* fast floating-point unit — and:

<!-- block: quote source="ch-02.md 2.3" -->
Thus, independent of K, the maximum speedup is 1.67.

<!-- block: prose -->
**1.67×, for a perfect floating-point unit.** The 60% you did not touch decides
the answer. This is the single most useful habit the chapter teaches: before
optimising anything, find out what fraction of the time it accounts for.

## Two product lines, two business models

The syllabus asks you to trace both, and the interesting difference is not
technical.

**Intel x86** is one company designing, manufacturing and selling the chips —
8086 through 80386, Pentium, Core, and on. Its defining constraint is
**backward compatibility**: every generation still runs the software of the one
before, which is the architecture/organization distinction from Stage 01 turned
into a forty-year commercial promise.

**ARM** works the other way:

<!-- block: quote source="ch-01.md 1.6" -->
ARM is a family of RISC-based microprocessors and microcontrollers designed by ARM Holdings, Cambridge, England. The company doesn't make processors but instead designs microprocessor and multicore architectures and licenses them to manufacturers.

<!-- block: prose -->
ARM sells the *architecture* — the contract — and lets licensees build their own
organization behind it. Two licensees can ship wildly different silicon that
runs the same instructions.

That is Stage 01's distinction as a business model, and it is why ARM dominates
where x86 does not: phones, tablets, and **embedded systems**, where the
processor is one part of a device rather than the point of it.

<!-- block: callout -->
**An embedded system is defined by its relationship to the world**, not by its
size. The textbook: such systems are typically *reactive* — in continual
interaction with the environment, executing at a pace the environment sets,
through sensors and actuators. A desktop waits for you. An engine controller
does not get to wait.

Their organization reflects it: processor and memory, plus A/D and D/A
conversion, custom logic, a diagnostic port, and a human interface that may be
nothing more than one flashing light.

<!-- block: prose -->
## The trap in comparing machines

MIPS is a rate of *instructions*, and instructions differ between architectures.
A machine that needs three instructions where another needs one can post a
higher MIPS number while doing less work. The same applies to MFLOPS across
different problem sizes.

This is why **benchmarks** exist — SPEC and friends run real programs and report
time-to-completion, which cannot be gamed by making instructions smaller. And it
is why the mean you choose matters: the book spends a section on arithmetic
versus harmonic versus geometric means, because averaging *rates* with the
arithmetic mean gives you the wrong answer.
