<!-- block: brief -->
For decades processors grew more complicated: more instructions, more addressing
modes, more special cases. Stage 10's `ENTER` instruction was one result: a
single instruction that did the work of three, and ran slower than them.

The **reduced instruction set computer** went the other way. It started not
from what a designer could add, but from what real programs actually spend
their time doing, and it built the simplest machine that does that fast. This
stage follows that argument from the measurements to the machine: the
evidence, the register file it led to, the compiler's part, and the pipeline
everything is designed around.

<!-- block: quote source="ch-15.md 15" -->
Although RISC architectures have been defined and designed in a variety of ways by different groups, the key elements shared by most designs are these:

<!-- block: prose -->
**A large number of general-purpose registers, or a compiler that makes the most
of a few; a limited and simple instruction set; and an emphasis on optimizing
the instruction pipeline.** Keep those three in view: every section below is one
of them.

<!-- block: prose -->
## What programs actually do

### The semantic gap

High-level languages kept getting more powerful, and hardware designers tried
to meet them halfway.

<!-- block: quote source="ch-15.md 15.1" -->
Alas, this solution gave rise to a perceived problem, known as the semantic gap, the difference between the operations provided in HLLs and those provided in computer architecture.

<!-- block: quote source="ch-15.md 15.1" -->
Designers responded with architectures intended to close this gap. Key features include large instruction sets, dozens of addressing modes, and various HLL statements implemented in hardware.

<!-- block: prose -->
The RISC researchers asked a different question first: which operations do
compiled programs **execute**, and how often? The studies counted while programs
ran, not by reading their source:

<!-- block: quote source="ch-15.md 15.1" -->
That is, measurements are collected by executing the program and counting the number of times some feature has appeared or a particular property has held true. In contrast, static measurements merely perform these counts on the source text of a program.

<!-- block: prose -->
### Operations

<!-- block: quote source="ch-15.md 15.1" -->
Assignment statements predominate, suggesting that the simple movement of data is of high importance. There is also a preponderance of conditional statements (IF, LOOP).

<!-- block: prose -->
Counting statements is not the same as counting time. When the Patterson study
weighted each kind of statement by the machine instructions and memory
references it compiles into, one kind stood out:

<!-- block: quote source="ch-15.md 15.1" -->
The results suggest that the procedure call/return is the most time-consuming operation in typical HLL programs.

<!-- block: callout -->
**The Patterson study, weighted** (book Table 15.2, Pascal and C). A CALL is
15% and 12% of statements executed, but 31% and 33% of machine instructions,
and 44% and 45% of memory references. Assignments are 45% and 38% of
statements but only 13% of instructions. Frequency and cost are different
questions.

<!-- block: prose -->
### Operands

<!-- block: quote source="ch-15.md 15.1" -->
The results, consistent between Pascal and C programs, show that most references are to simple scalar variables. Further, more than 80% of the scalars were local (to the procedure) variables.

<!-- block: prose -->
### Procedure calls

How many values does a procedure need? Very few:

<!-- block: quote source="ch-15.md 15.1" -->
Tanenbaum’s study [TANE78] found that 98% of dynamically called procedures were passed fewer than six arguments and that 92% of them used fewer than six local scalar variables.

<!-- block: quote source="ch-15.md 15.1" -->
Rather, they found that a program remains confined to a rather narrow window of procedure-invocation depth.

<!-- block: prose -->
### What the measurements imply

Put those together: programs move data constantly, mostly local scalars, a few
at a time per procedure, and they call and return a great deal without nesting
very deep. Three design rules follow, and they are the three elements above:

<!-- block: quote source="ch-15.md 15.1" -->
This, coupled with the locality and predominance of scalar references, suggests that performance can be improved by reducing memory references at the expense of more register references.

<!-- block: quote source="ch-15.md 15.1" -->
Because of the high proportion of conditional branch and procedure call instructions, a straightforward instruction pipeline will be inefficient.

<!-- block: quote source="ch-15.md 15.1" -->
Finally, an instruction set consisting of high-performance primitives is indicated.

<!-- block: prose -->
## A large register file

Registers are the fastest storage there is (Stage 04 and Stage 12), so keep the
busy operands in them. There are two ways to do it:

<!-- block: quote source="ch-15.md 15.2" -->
The software approach is to rely on the compiler to maximize register usage.

<!-- block: quote source="ch-15.md 15.2" -->
The hardware approach is simply to use more registers so that more variables can be held in registers for longer periods of time.

<!-- block: prose -->
### Register windows

The difficulty with many registers is the procedure call. Every call changes
which variables are "local", so the old locals must be saved to memory and the
parameters passed; every return reverses it. The measurements above say each
procedure needs only a few registers and nesting stays shallow, so:

<!-- block: quote source="ch-15.md 15.2" -->
A procedure call automatically switches the processor to use a different fixed-size window of registers, rather than saving registers in memory. Windows for adjacent procedures are overlapped to allow parameter passing.

<!-- block: prose -->
Each window has three parts: **parameter** registers (passed in from the
caller, and results passed back), **local** registers, and **temporary**
registers (used to talk to the next procedure down). The trick is the overlap:

<!-- block: figure id="13-register-windows" after="15.1" -->
Two windows, one for a procedure at level J and one for the procedure it calls.
The caller's temporary registers are the callee's parameter registers.

<!-- block: quote source="ch-15.md 15.2" -->
The temporary registers at one level are physically the same as the parameter registers at the next lower level. This overlap permits parameters to be passed without the actual movement of data.

<!-- block: prose -->
The windows form a **circular buffer**: a current-window pointer moves one
window on each call and back on each return. Only when the buffer is full (a
call deeper than the buffer holds) is the oldest window saved to memory, and
only when a return reaches a saved window is it restored.

<!-- block: quote source="ch-15.md 15.2" -->
From the preceding, it can be seen that an N-window register file can hold only N - 1 procedure activations. The value of N need not be large.

<!-- block: quote source="ch-15.md 15.2" -->
As was mentioned in Appendix 4A, one study [TAMI83] found that, with 8 windows, a save or restore is needed on only 1% of the calls or returns.

<!-- block: prose -->
**Global variables** do not fit the scheme, since every procedure must see the
same copy. Either the compiler keeps them in memory, or the processor adds a
small set of global registers that every window shares.

### Large register file versus cache

A window file is a fast buffer for the variables most in use, which sounds like
a cache. The book's comparison (Table 15.5): the register file holds **all**
local scalars of the recent procedures and is managed by call depth; a cache
holds **recently used** values in blocks and is managed by its replacement
algorithm. The cache uses space better and handles globals naturally, but the
register file wins on one point that decides it:

<!-- block: quote source="ch-15.md 15.2" -->
It should be clear that even if the cache is as fast as the register file, the access time will be considerably longer.

<!-- block: prose -->
A register is selected by a small register number and a window number through a
simple decoder; a cache needs a full address, a tag comparison and a select.

<!-- block: quote source="ch-15.md 15.2" -->
Thus, from the point of view of performance, the window-based register file is superior for local scalars.

<!-- block: prose -->
## Compiler-based register optimization

With only 16 to 32 registers, the work moves to the compiler. It first gives
every value a **symbolic register**, as many as it likes, and then fits them
into the real ones:

<!-- block: quote source="ch-15.md 15.3" -->
The compiler then maps the unlimited number of symbolic registers into a fixed number of real registers. Symbolic registers whose usage does not overlap can share the same real register.

<!-- block: quote source="ch-15.md 15.3" -->
The technique most commonly used in RISC compilers is known as graph coloring, which is a technique borrowed from the discipline of topology [CHAI82, CHOW86, COUT86, CHOW90].

<!-- block: quote source="ch-15.md 15.3" -->
The nodes of the graph are the symbolic registers. If two symbolic registers are “live” during the same program fragment, then they are joined by an edge to depict interference. An attempt is then made to color the graph with n colors, where n is the number of registers.

<!-- block: prose -->
Two symbolic registers joined by an edge are in use at the same time, so they
cannot share a real register. Our own example, with six symbolic registers and
three real ones:

<!-- block: figure id="13-graph-coloring" -->
A register interference graph coloured with three registers. A and D share R1,
B and E share R2, C has R3. F is joined to registers holding all three, so it
cannot be coloured and is kept in memory.

<!-- block: prose -->
Check it yourself: no line joins two nodes with the same register, and F's four
neighbours already use R1, R2 and R3. That is what the book means when a node
"cannot be colored":

<!-- block: quote source="ch-15.md 15.3" -->
If this process does not fully succeed, then those nodes that cannot be colored must be placed in memory, and loads and stores must be used to make space for the affected quantities when they are needed.

<!-- block: prose -->
How many registers are worth having, then? The study the book cites is
reassuring for small register files:

<!-- block: quote source="ch-15.md 15.3" -->
Their study showed that with even simple register optimization, there is little benefit to the use of more than 64 registers. With reasonably sophisticated register optimization techniques, there is only marginal performance improvement with more than 32 registers.

<!-- block: prose -->
## The reduced instruction set

### Why CISC, and why that reasoning was doubted

Complex instruction sets were built for two reasons: to make compilers simpler,
and to make programs smaller and faster. The RISC researchers questioned both.
On compilers:

<!-- block: quote source="ch-15.md 15.4" -->
They have found that complex machine instructions are often hard to exploit because the compiler must find those cases that exactly fit the construct.

<!-- block: prose -->
On size, a CISC program has fewer instructions but not necessarily fewer bits,
because more opcodes need longer opcode fields and memory operands need longer
address fields. The book's Table 15.6 found little or no saving over the RISC I.
On speed, a richer instruction set makes the control unit or the microprogram
store bigger, which slows down the simple instructions programs mostly use.

### The four characteristics

<!-- block: callout -->
**What reduced instruction set architectures share** (book §15.4): **one
instruction per cycle** · **register-to-register operations**, with only load and
store touching memory · **simple addressing modes** · **simple instruction
formats**, few of them, of fixed length.

<!-- block: quote source="ch-15.md 15.4" -->
A machine cycle is defined to be the time it takes to fetch two operands from registers, perform an ALU operation, and store the result in a register.

<!-- block: quote source="ch-15.md 15.4" -->
With simple, one-cycle instructions, there is little or no need for microcode; the machine instructions can be hardwired.

<!-- block: quote source="ch-15.md 15.4" -->
For example, a RISC instruction set may include only one or two ADD instructions (e.g., integer add, add with carry); the VAX has 25 different ADD instructions.

<!-- block: quote source="ch-15.md 15.4" -->
Instruction length is fixed and aligned on word boundaries. Field locations, especially the opcode, are fixed. This design feature has a number of benefits. With fixed fields, opcode decoding and register operand accessing can occur simultaneously.

<!-- block: prose -->
That last point connects back to Stage 11: a fixed format means the decoder
never has to discover where a field is, so decoding and register reading can
happen in the same moment.

### RISC versus CISC, today

<!-- block: quote source="ch-15.md 15.4" -->
After the initial enthusiasm for RISC machines, there has been a growing realization that (1) RISC designs may benefit from the inclusion of some CISC features and that (2) CISC designs may benefit from the inclusion of some RISC features.

<!-- block: quote source="ch-15.md 15.8" -->
In more recent years, the RISC versus CISC controversy has died down to a great extent. This is because there has been a gradual convergence of the technologies.

<!-- block: prose -->
## RISC pipelining

Most RISC instructions are register to register and need just two stages: **I**
(fetch) and **E** (execute: an ALU operation, registers in, register out). Loads
and stores need a third, **D** (the memory access). Because every instruction is
simple and about the same length of work, the stages can be made about equal,
which is what a pipeline wants (Stage 12):

<!-- block: quote source="ch-15.md 15.5" -->
Because of the simple and regular nature of RISC instructions, it is easier for a hardware designer to implement a simple, fast pipeline. There are few variations in instruction execution duration, and the pipeline can be tailored to reflect this.

<!-- block: prose -->
The book builds up from two stages to three (two memory accesses per stage) to
four (E split into E1, register read, and E2, ALU and register write), each step
overlapping more instructions. What still breaks the flow are Stage 12's data
and branch dependencies, and a RISC pipeline's simplest answer is to insert a
**NOOP**, an instruction that does nothing, rather than build hardware to stall.

### Delayed branch

The better answer is to put a useful instruction where the NOOP would go.

<!-- block: quote source="ch-15.md 15.5" -->
Delayed branch, a way of increasing the efficiency of the pipeline, makes use of a branch that does not take effect until after execution of the following instruction (hence the term delayed). The instruction location immediately following the branch is referred to as the delay slot.

<!-- block: prose -->
Our own example. A jump has an instruction before it that it does not depend
on. Instead of filling the slot with a NOOP, the compiler swaps them:

<!-- block: code -->
normal (slot filled by a NOOP)      delayed branch, optimized
LOAD  X, rA                         LOAD  X, rA
ADD   1, rA                         JUMP  TARGET
JUMP  TARGET                        ADD   1, rA        ← runs in the delay slot
NOOP                                ...
...
TARGET: STORE rA, Z                 TARGET: STORE rA, Z

<!-- block: prose -->
The `ADD` is fetched before the jump changes the program counter, so it still
runs, and the program means the same while doing one less useless cycle. The
condition for the swap matters:

<!-- block: quote source="ch-15.md 15.5" -->
This interchange of instructions will work successfully for unconditional branches, calls, and returns. For conditional branches, this procedure cannot be blindly applied.

<!-- block: prose -->
If the condition the branch tests can be altered by the

<!-- block: quote source="ch-15.md 15.5" -->
immediately preceding instruction, then the compiler must refrain from doing the interchange and instead insert a NOOP. Otherwise, the compiler can seek to insert a useful instruction after the branch.

<!-- block: prose -->
Even so, the Berkeley RISC and IBM 801 experience was that most conditional
branches can be optimized this way.

### Delayed load

<!-- block: quote source="ch-15.md 15.5" -->
On LOAD instructions, the register that is to be the target of the load is locked by the processor. The processor then continues execution of the instruction stream until it reaches an instruction requiring that register, at which point it idles until the load is complete.

<!-- block: prose -->
So the compiler moves independent instructions between a load and the first use
of its register, and the wait disappears into useful work.

### Loop unrolling

<!-- block: quote source="ch-15.md 15.5" -->
Unrolling replicates the body of a loop some number of times called the unrolling factor (u) and iterates by step u instead of step 1.

<!-- block: prose -->
Unrolled twice, a loop does two iterations' work before its test and branch, so
the loop overhead is halved; the two bodies can overlap in the pipeline; and
values shared by both bodies stay in registers. Our own sketch:

<!-- block: code -->
for i = 0 to 99:            for i = 0 to 99 step 2:
    a[i] = a[i] + s             a[i]   = a[i]   + s
                                a[i+1] = a[i+1] + s
100 tests and branches      50 tests and branches

<!-- block: prose -->
(With an odd count, one leftover iteration is done after the loop.)

## What to carry into Stage 14

You can now say what measurements of real programs motivated RISC, list its
key characteristics, explain how overlapping register windows pass parameters
without moving data and why they beat a cache for local scalars, colour a
register interference graph, and name the RISC answers to pipeline hazards:
NOOPs, delayed branch, delayed load and loop unrolling.

Stage 14 takes the pipeline one step further: if one instruction can start every
cycle, why not several? That is **instruction-level parallelism** and the
**superscalar** processor.
