<!-- block: brief -->
Every stage so far has moved data around: into caches, across buses, out to
disks, in and out of memory pages. This one finally asks what happens to it at
the centre of the machine — in the **arithmetic and logic unit**, where numbers
are actually added, subtracted, multiplied and divided.

Two questions run through the whole chapter, and you should keep them apart:
how a number is **represented** in bits, and what **algorithm** the hardware
uses to do arithmetic on that representation. A good representation makes the
algorithms cheap. That is the whole reason two's complement won.

<!-- block: callout -->
**Number systems are assumed knowledge.** Binary, hexadecimal and conversion
between them come from your Microprocessors prerequisite; book chapter 9 is the
refresher if you need it. This chapter starts from there.

<!-- block: prose -->
## The ALU, where the work is done

<!-- block: quote source="ch-10.md 10.1" -->
The ALU is that part of the computer that actually performs arithmetic and logical operations on data.

<!-- block: prose -->
Operands arrive in registers, the result goes back to a register, and the ALU
also sets **flags** — overflow, carry, zero, sign — that later instructions can
test. The control unit decides which operation happens. Everything in Stages 03
to 08 exists, in the end, to feed this one unit.

<!-- block: prose -->
## Representing integers

An unsigned 8-bit word holds 0 to 255. The interesting problem is **negative**
numbers, because a word has no spare symbol for a minus sign. Every scheme
spends the leftmost bit as a sign bit; they differ in how they read the rest.

### Sign-magnitude, and why it lost

The obvious scheme: the leftmost bit is the sign, the other bits are the size.
+18 is `00010010` and −18 is `10010010`.

<!-- block: quote source="ch-10.md 10.2" -->
There are several drawbacks to sign-magnitude representation. One is that addition and subtraction require a consideration of both the signs of the numbers and their relative magnitudes to carry out the required operation.

<!-- block: prose -->
It also has **two zeros**, `00000000` and `10000000`, so even the simple question
"is this zero?" needs two comparisons.

<!-- block: quote source="ch-10.md 10.2" -->
Because of these drawbacks, sign-magnitude representation is rarely used in implementing the integer portion of the ALU. Instead, the most common scheme is twos complement representation.

<!-- block: prose -->
### Two's complement

<!-- block: quote source="ch-10.md 10.2" -->
Like sign magnitude, twos complement representation uses the most significant bit as a sign bit, making it easy to test whether an integer is positive or negative.

<!-- block: prose -->
The difference is what the leftmost bit is **worth**. In an n-bit word it
counts as **−2ⁿ⁻¹** instead of +2ⁿ⁻¹, and every other bit keeps its ordinary
positive weight. Read a pattern as a "value box":

<!-- block: code -->
weight:   −128   64   32   16    8    4    2    1
bits:        1    1    0    1    0    0    1    1
value:    −128 + 64      + 16          + 2  + 1  =  −45

<!-- block: prose -->
That one rule explains everything else:

- **One zero.** All bits clear is zero, and nothing else is.
- **The range is lopsided.** n bits hold −2ⁿ⁻¹ to 2ⁿ⁻¹ − 1. In 4 bits, −8 to
  +7; in 8 bits, −128 to +127. There is one more negative number than positive.
- **The sign bit still tells you the sign**, because the leftmost bit's
  negative weight outweighs all the others put together.

<!-- block: prose -->
### Negation: flip, then add one

<!-- block: code -->
+45          = 0010 1101
flip all     = 1101 0010
add 1        = 1101 0011   = −45   (check it with the value box above)

<!-- block: quote source="ch-10.md 10.3" -->
This two-step process is referred to as the twos complement operation, or the taking of the twos complement of an integer.

<!-- block: callout -->
**Two things that trip people up.** Negating 0 gives 0 again (the carry out of
the top is thrown away). And negating the most negative number gives it back
unchanged: in 8 bits, −128 has no +128 to become. That is the price of having
one zero.

<!-- block: prose -->
### Range extension: copying the sign

Storing an 8-bit value in 16 bits cannot simply pad with zeros — that would turn
every negative number into a large positive one.

<!-- block: quote source="ch-10.md 10.2" -->
Instead, the rule for twos complement integers is to move the sign bit to the new leftmost position and fill in with copies of the sign bit. For positive numbers, fill in with zeros, and for negative numbers, fill in with ones. This is called sign extension.

<!-- block: code -->
−5 in 4 bits :            1011
−5 in 8 bits :       1111 1011
+5 in 4 bits :            0101
+5 in 8 bits :       0000 0101

<!-- block: prose -->
## Integer arithmetic

### Addition and subtraction — the reason two's complement won

Two's complement numbers are added **exactly as if they were unsigned**. The
same adder circuit serves both, with no special case for signs. Any carry out of
the top bit is simply discarded.

Subtraction needs no subtractor at all:

<!-- block: quote source="ch-10.md 10.3" -->
subtraction rule: To subtract one number (subtrahend) from another (minuend), take the twos complement (negation) of the subtrahend and add it to the minuend.

<!-- block: code -->
7 − 5  in 4 bits
  0111            7
+ 1011           −5   (0101 flipped = 1010, plus 1)
──────
1 0010            2   (the carry out is discarded)

<!-- block: prose -->
So the hardware is one adder and one complementer in front of it, with a switch
choosing whether the second operand passes through the complementer.

### Overflow

The result can be too big for the word. The ALU must notice and say so through
the overflow flag.

<!-- block: quote source="ch-10.md 10.3" -->
overflow rule: If two numbers are added, and they are both positive or both negative, then overflow occurs if and only if the result has the opposite sign.

<!-- block: code -->
+5 + +4  in 4 bits (range −8 … +7)
  0101
+ 0100
──────
  1001     reads as −7   ← two positives gave a negative: OVERFLOW

<!-- block: callout -->
**Overflow is not carry.** A carry out of the top bit is normal in two's
complement and means nothing on its own (7 − 5 above produced one and was
correct). Overflow is about the **signs**: adding two numbers of the same sign
and getting the other sign. Adding a positive and a negative can never overflow.

<!-- block: prose -->
### Multiplication

Pencil-and-paper binary multiplication is easy: for each 1 in the multiplier,
add the multiplicand shifted into place; for each 0, add nothing. The product of
two n-bit numbers needs up to 2n bits. Hardware does the same with a running
sum: test the multiplier's lowest bit, add or not, shift right, repeat n times.

That simple method is right for unsigned numbers and **wrong for two's
complement**, because the multiplier's top bit is worth a negative amount. The
standard fix is **Booth's algorithm**, which looks at the multiplier in runs of
1s. A run of ones is a difference of two powers of two:

<!-- block: code -->
multiplier 0011110  =  16 + 8 + 4 + 2           (four additions)
                    =  32 − 2                   (one subtraction, one addition)

<!-- block: quote source="ch-10.md 10.3" -->
Booth’s algorithm conforms to this scheme by performing a subtraction when the first 1 of the block is encountered (1–0) and an addition when the end of the block is encountered (0–1).

<!-- block: prose -->
It handles negative multipliers correctly without any conversion, and it skips
whole runs of identical bits, so it is often faster too. Its shifts must keep the
sign:

<!-- block: quote source="ch-10.md 10.3" -->
This is required to preserve the sign of the number in A and Q. It is known as an arithmetic shift, because it preserves the sign bit.

<!-- block: prose -->
### Division

Division is long division in binary: shift, try subtracting the divisor, keep
the result if it is not negative (a quotient bit of 1), otherwise restore the
old value (a quotient bit of 0). That *restore* step names the method,
**restoring division**. For signed operands the usual approach divides the
magnitudes and fixes the signs at the end: the remainder takes the dividend's
sign, and the quotient is negative when the signs differ.

## Floating-point representation

Fixed-point integers cannot hold very large or very small values. Scientific
notation solves that for decimal, and floating point is the same idea in binary.

<!-- block: quote source="ch-10.md 10.4" -->
What we have done, in effect, is dynamically to slide the decimal point to a convenient location and use the exponent of 10 to keep track of that decimal point.

<!-- block: prose -->
A floating-point number is **± significand × base^exponent**. The base is fixed
(2) and never stored, so a word holds three fields: a **sign** bit, a biased
**exponent**, and the **significand**.

### The biased exponent

<!-- block: quote source="ch-10.md 10.4" -->
The exponent value is stored in the next 8 bits. The representation used is known as a biased representation. A fixed value, called the bias, is subtracted from the field to get the true exponent value.

<!-- block: prose -->
With 8 bits and a bias of 127, a stored 129 means a true exponent of 2. Bias
makes exponents compare like plain unsigned integers, so two positive
floating-point numbers can be compared with integer hardware.

### Normalised significands and the hidden bit

<!-- block: quote source="ch-10.md 10.4" -->
For base 2 representation, a normal number is therefore one in which the most significant bit of the significand is one.

<!-- block: prose -->
If the leading bit is always 1, there is no point storing it. The 23-bit field
holds the bits **after** the binary point, and the 1 in front is implied — a free
extra bit of precision.

<!-- block: code -->
−6.25 as IEEE 754 binary32
  6.25   = 110.01₂ = 1.1001₂ × 2²
  sign   = 1                         (negative)
  exp    = 2 + 127 = 129 = 1000 0001
  frac   = 1001 0000 0000 0000 0000 000   (the leading 1 is implied)
  word   = 1 10000001 10010000000000000000000  =  0xC0C80000

<!-- block: prose -->
### Range, precision, and what floating point does not buy

<!-- block: quote source="ch-10.md 10.4" -->
It is important to note that we are not representing more individual values with floating-point notation.

<!-- block: prose -->
32 bits are still 32 bits. Floating point spreads the same number of values
over a vast range, crowded near zero and sparse far from it, so most real
numbers are only approximated.

<!-- block: quote source="ch-10.md 10.4" -->
If we increase the number of bits in the exponent, we expand the range of expressible numbers. But because only a fixed number of different values can be expressed, we have reduced the density of those numbers and therefore the precision. The only way to increase both range and precision is to use more bits.

<!-- block: prose -->
## The IEEE 754 standard

<!-- block: quote source="ch-10.md 10.4" -->
The most important floating-point representation is defined in IEEE Standard 754, adopted in 1985 and revised in 2008.

<!-- block: quote source="ch-10.md 10.4" -->
The three basic binary formats have bit lengths of 32, 64, and 128 bits, with exponents of 8, 11, and 15 bits, respectively (Figure 10.21).

<!-- block: callout -->
**The three basic binary formats** (book Table 10.3):

| | binary32 | binary64 | binary128 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Exponent bits | 8 | 11 | 15 |
| Bias | 127 | 1023 | 16383 |
| Stored significand bits | 23 | 52 | 112 |

<!-- block: prose -->
The exponent field's two extreme values are reserved for special cases:

- **All zeros, fraction zero:** ±0 (the sign bit gives the sign).
- **All ones, fraction zero:** ±infinity, so an overflow can carry on as ∞
  instead of stopping the program.
- **All ones, fraction not zero:** **NaN**, "not a number", the result of
  things like 0 × ∞ or ∞ − ∞.
- **All zeros, fraction not zero:** a **subnormal** number, below.

<!-- block: prose -->
## Floating-point arithmetic

Multiplication and division are the easy ones: add (or subtract) the exponents,
removing the doubled bias, then multiply (or divide) the significands, normalise
and round. **Addition and subtraction are harder**, because the exponents must
match first.

<!-- block: quote source="ch-10.md 10.5" -->
Alignment may be achieved by shifting either the smaller number to the right (increasing its exponent) or shifting the larger number to the left.

<!-- block: code -->
1.100₂ × 2³ + 1.000₂ × 2¹        (12 + 2)
align: 1.000₂ × 2¹ = 0.010₂ × 2³
add:   1.100 + 0.010 = 1.110₂ × 2³   = 14
normalise (already normal), round, done

<!-- block: prose -->
The smaller number is the one shifted, so the bits that fall off are the least
significant ones. Shift far enough and nothing is left:

<!-- block: quote source="ch-10.md 10.5" -->
Thus, if two numbers have exponents that differ significantly, the lesser number is lost.

<!-- block: prose -->
## Precision: guard bits, rounding, subnormals

### Guard bits

<!-- block: quote source="ch-10.md 10.5" -->
The register contains additional bits, called guard bits, which are used to pad out the right end of the significand with 0s.

<!-- block: prose -->
Bits shifted out during alignment land in the guard bits instead of vanishing,
so a subtraction of two nearly equal numbers keeps its last bit of significance.

### Rounding

When the extra bits are dropped, the result must be rounded. IEEE 754 offers
four modes: round to nearest (the default), round toward +∞, round toward −∞,
and round toward 0 (plain truncation). The subtle case is a result exactly
halfway between two representable values:

<!-- block: quote source="ch-10.md 10.5" -->
The approach taken by the IEEE standard is to force the result to be even: If the result of a computation is exactly midway between two representable numbers, the value is rounded up if the last representable bit is currently 1 and not rounded up if it is currently 0.

<!-- block: prose -->
Rounding the tie to even means half the ties go up and half go down, so no
steady bias builds up over millions of operations — which truncation, always
toward zero, would cause.

### Underflow and subnormal numbers

**Exponent overflow** is a result too large for the exponent field;
**exponent underflow** is one too close to zero. Without help, there is a sudden
gap between the smallest normal number and zero.

<!-- block: quote source="ch-10.md 10.5" -->
Subnormal numbers are included in IEEE 754 to handle cases of exponent underflow.

<!-- block: prose -->
A subnormal has an all-zeros exponent field and gives up the implied leading 1,
so it can be smaller than any normal number at the cost of precision.

<!-- block: quote source="ch-10.md 10.5" -->
Gradual underflow fills in that gap and reduces the impact of exponent underflow to a level comparable with roundoff among the normal numbers.
